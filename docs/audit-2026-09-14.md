# Factual audit — 14 September 2026

What was checked, against which source, and what changed. Every verdict below
came from a page or dataset actually opened; nothing rests on a search-engine
summary. `UNVERIFIED` appears where no source could be opened, and is left as
`UNVERIFIED` rather than guessed.

## Method

Three classes of claim in the atlas are falsifiable against a public register.
Each was checked against the register itself, downloaded whole, rather than
looked up item by item:

| class | count | source |
|---|---|---|
| protected designations | 176 | EU geographical-indications register (3 980 records) + Swiss federal AOP/IGP register (42) |
| latin names | 1 335 | GBIF taxonomic backbone |
| Michelin star claims | 45 | Wikipedia article text, read raw |

Each instrument was control-tested before its results were believed — it had to
go red on a known-bad case in the same run, not merely green on good ones.

- **Designations.** The matcher confirms Comté PDO, rejects Comté claimed as
  IGP, refuses bare "Coppa" where only "Coppa di Parma" is registered, and
  returns nothing for a name absent from the register.
- **Latin names.** `Tuber melanosporumm` returns no match where
  `Crocus sativus` matches exactly.
- **Star claims.** Read from the raw article text, not a summary. This matters:
  on the first pass a summariser answered "2019" for Alchemist's stars where the
  article gives no year at all.

## Flagged, not changed

### Swiss, not EU (8) — correct, worth a note

Emmentaler, Gruyère, L'Etivaz, Sbrinz, Tête de Moine, Vacherin fribourgeois,
Raclette du Valais, viande des Grisons (*Bündnerfleisch*). All confirmed in the
Swiss federal register. They are a national scheme, not the EU one — and the EU
register's *Gruyère* PGI is the **French** cheese, a different product from
Swiss Gruyère AOP.

### Spirits — your call (6)

Armagnac, Cognac, Calvados, Marc de Bourgogne, Grappa, Brandy de Jerez carry
AOP or IGP badges. The EU register classes all six as spirit-drink
**geographical indications** (GI), not PDO/PGI. French usage calls Cognac and
Armagnac **AOC**. The badge vocabulary has no slot for either. I did not invent
one.

### Styles within an appellation — kept

Ruby / tawny / white port, fino, amontillado, oloroso, Pedro Ximénez, Marsala
dolce and secco, Madeira Malmsey and Sercial, Rivesaltes ambré, Asiago d'allevo,
Gorgonzola dolce and piccante. Each is a named type inside a registered
appellation — a tawny port is a Porto PDO wine. The badges stand.

### Latin names

889 of 1 335 matched the backbone exactly as currently accepted names. No bulk
edit was made, and none is recommended: the backbone's "synonym" list is mostly
its own lumping of infraspecific ranks (*Brassica oleracea* var. *italica* →
*Brassica oleracea*), and its accepted names for several kitchen staples —
*Lathyrus oleraceus* for the pea, *Salvia rosmarinus* for rosemary — would make
a cookery atlas worse, not better.

Worth a look, all small:

- *Schinus terebinthifolius* → *Schinus terebinthifolia* (pink peppercorn) —
  gender agreement; the backbone marks the corrected form ACCEPTED.
- *Ocimum basilicum* var. *thyrsiflora* → *thyrsiflorum* (Thai basil) — same.
- *Scapharca broughtonii* → *Anadara broughtonii* (akagai).
- *Lobatus gigas* → *Aliger gigas* (conch).
- *Morchella conica* → the backbone folds it into *M. esculenta*; the black
  morel's name is genuinely contested.
- *Tuber uncinatum* → *Tuber aestivum*; the burgundy truffle is now read as a
  form of the summer truffle.

**One caution about this instrument.** GBIF's matcher does not resolve
*Prunus dulcis* — the almond — falling back to the genus. So a `NONE` or
`HIGHERRANK` result is not evidence of an error, and the 351 such rows are
reported as inconclusive rather than as findings. Most of them are the atlas's
culinary Latin (*Butyrum clarificatum*, *Acetum vini*), which no taxonomic
backbone contains by design.

### Origins

For the 106 products the EU register confirms, the register also names the
country. 28 atlas entries name a country outright in their origin line, and
**none contradicts the register**. The other 78 name a region instead —
"Roquefort-sur-Soulzon, Aveyron" rather than "France" — which is more precise,
not less, so no flag. The check was controlled: a planted "Somewhere in Italy"
against a France registration fires it.

### Price bands

Bands are internally coherent: medians rise 7.5 → 22.5 → 60 → 375 €/kg. Three
records sit outside their band — phycocyanine (band 3, sold by the gram, which
is band 4's own definition), benitade and labrador tea (band 2 at 150–300 €/kg,
where band 3's median is 60). The band is an editorial scale, so these are
listed, not changed.

## The prose — second pass

The first pass stopped at the register-checkable fields. This pass covers what
is actually written in the entries: 1.87 million characters of story, tip and
origin across 1 838 ingredients.

### Bilingual cross-check — clean

Every entry is written twice, in English and French, which makes the atlas its
own control. A year stated in one language and contradicted in the other is an
error findable with no outside source at all.

**Zero contradictions.** Across all 1 838 entries: no disagreeing years, no
disagreeing centuries, no disagreeing temperatures, no disagreeing percentages.

That number is only worth reading because the check was proven to fire. A first
version returned 262 "mismatches" that were all paraphrase — English "in the
1920s" against French "dans les années 1920", "nineteenth-century" against
"XIXe siècle", "1.5" against "1,5". After normalising those forms the check
passes four controls: it stays silent on a faithful translation, on a
paraphrased decade and on a spelled-out century, and it fires on a planted
1915-against-1925.

### One error, corrected

**Capers.** The entry said the caper harvest on Pantelleria is a UNESCO-listed
art. It is not. UNESCO's own listing for that island is the *vite ad alberello*
— the head-trained bush vines — inscribed in 2014. The capers of Pantelleria do
hold a distinction, but a different one: *Cappero di Pantelleria*, PGI-IT-0307,
registered 21 June 1996. The sentence now says that instead, in both languages.

Source opened: the [UNESCO inscription](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/traditional-agricultural-practice-of-cultivating-the-vite-ad-alberello-head-trained-bush-vines-of-the-community-of-pantelleria-00720).

### One imprecision, flagged not changed

**Red yeast rice** reads: "The mould makes monacolin K, a molecule chemically
identical to lovastatin — the first statin ever brought to market came out of
this ferment." Lovastatin was indeed the first statin approved by the FDA, in
1987, but it was isolated from *Aspergillus terreus*. The molecule occurs in
both organisms; the drug did not come out of this ferment. The fix is a clause,
and it is your sentence to rewrite.

### What was sampled, and what held

Claims were sorted by how badly they could be wrong. Sensory description cannot
be false; statements about law, attributions to named people, and world-scale
superlatives can, and those are what a student repeats.

| class | instances | entries |
|---|---|---|
| superlative | 336 | 301 |
| quantified | 485 | 356 |
| dated | 457 | 390 |
| attribution | 36 | 36 |
| law | 34 | 32 |
| institution | 17 | 15 |

860 of 1 838 entries carry at least one checkable claim.

Sampled against opened pages, and confirmed: the tonka ban (FDA, adulterated
since 1954), the blackcurrant ban (US disease vector, federal ban lifted 1966,
some state bans still standing), water spinach prohibited in Florida since 1973,
Da Hong Pao's six mother bushes closed to picking by the Wuyi government in
2006, jamón serrano's TSG status, Mont d'Or's legally closed season, ramps
protected under Quebec legislation, kimjang and couscous on the UNESCO list,
edible gold as E175, Martinique rhum agricole as the only AOC of its kind
(November 1996), and macvin as the Jura mistelle muted with marc rather than
wine brandy.

One nuance worth knowing rather than fixing: **absinthe**. The entry says France
banned it in 1915, which matches the law of 16 March 1915. Wikipedia's article
says 1914, which is the wartime decree that suspended sale first. Both dates are
defensible; the entry is not wrong.

### Coverage — stated plainly

This is a sample, not a sweep. Around 300 000 words of prose exist and a few
dozen claims were opened against sources. The bilingual check is the only thing
here that ran across all 1 838 entries, and it only catches a claim that is
wrong in one language — not one wrong in both.

Unverified and left standing, as examples of what a full pass would still have
to reach: the 1947 US court ruling that made rhubarb a fruit, the FDA's 1982
pink-peppercorn import ban, the Savoie decree of June 2021 capping génépi at 120
sprigs, the 1993 decree behind pain de tradition française, Nyons as the first
French olive appellation in 1994, and Bella di Cerignola as the largest table
olive in commerce.

## Not covered

Individual verification of the remaining prose claims. The classes are counted
above and the register-free ones — French decrees especially — need Legifrance
rather than an encyclopedia.

# Follow-up, 15 September 2026 — the flagged items shipped

Everything the first pass flagged has now been either changed with its source
logged, or left alone with a stated reason.

## Changed

**The six spirits now carry the designation they actually hold.** Cognac,
Armagnac, Calvados and Marc de Bourgogne read **AOC**; Grappa and Brandy de
Jerez read **IG**. Verified on each product's own article: Cognac by the decree
of 15 May 1936, one of the first six French AOCs; Armagnac 1936; Calvados 1942;
Marc de Bourgogne by decree of 23 February 1942. Grappa is a protected
geographical indication reserved to Italy; Brandy de Jerez is a spirit-drink GI
in the EU register.

The badge vocabulary grew by two, and `signMark` now reads a geographical
indication (IGP, PGI, **IG**) as blue and an origin appellation (AOP, DOP, PDO,
**AOC**) as red. The filter label derives from the data, so it says
*Protected origin (AOC, AOP, DOP, IG, IGP, PDO)* on its own.

**Balsamic gets its IGP back.** Removing it last night was an overreach of mine.
At 5–12 €/L from Modena, with a separate entry already carrying the tradizionale
DOP, this entry is *Aceto Balsamico di Modena* IGP — the pair maps cleanly onto
IGP and DOP, and stripping one half broke that.

**Keller's stars were wrong, not merely incomplete.** The note said three stars
at The French Laundry and Per Se "in the same year". Per Se took three in 2005
and The French Laundry in 2006 — *"In 2005, he was awarded three stars in the
inaugural Michelin Guide for New York City for his restaurant Per Se, and in
2006 ... for the French Laundry."* Corrected in both languages.

**Red yeast rice** no longer says the first statin came out of this ferment.
Lovastatin was isolated from a different mould; the molecule occurs in both.

**Four latin names**, each marked accepted by the backbone: *Schinus
terebinthifolia*, *Ocimum basilicum* var. *thyrsiflorum*, *Anadara broughtonii*,
*Aliger gigas*.

**Three price bands** that contradicted their own printed range: phycocyanine
3 → 4 (sold by the gram, which is band 4's definition), benitade and labrador
tea 2 → 3 (150–300 €/kg against a band-2 median of 22).

## Two claims closed at the primary source

Légifrance renders in a browser where it refuses a plain fetch, and the decree
itself settles both flour claims.

**Décret n°93-1074 du 13 septembre 1993, article 2** — *pain de tradition
française* may contain no additive, may not be frozen at any stage, and its
dough must be *"composée exclusivement d'un mélange de farines panifiables de
blé, d'eau potable et de sel de cuisine"*, fermented with baker's yeast or
levain. Both `flour-t65` and the decree's scope are confirmed.

Article 2, 3° lists the adjuvants and their ceilings: **2 % fava flour**, 0.5 %
soya flour, 0.3 % wheat malt flour. `fava-bean-flour`'s claim — exactly three
adjuvants, fava at 2 %, first of them — is confirmed word for word.

## Left alone, with the reason

**The 16 "generic name" badges stay.** Checking each entry's origin line
dissolved the concern: every one is already scoped to the protected product —
bresaola to Valtellina, mortadella to Bologna, cotechino to Modena, sobrasada to
Mallorca, soppressata to Calabria, calçot to Valls, lonzu to Corsica, manchego
to La Mancha, vin santo to Tuscany. The entry names the region the designation
belongs to, so the badge is not claiming that any coppa is protected.

**The eight Swiss badges stay as AOP.** They are correct. Marking them
"Swiss AOP" would add a seventh token to the filter for no factual gain.

**Veyrat and Gagnaire stay as written.** Veyrat's 1995 and 2001 are both
confirmed; he took three stars a third time in 2018 and lost them in the 2019
edition, which is a sentence to add rather than a value to change. Gagnaire's
Saint-Étienne 1993 is confirmed and the Paris year is not stated anywhere I
could open.

**Still unverified:** the 1947 US court ruling on rhubarb, the FDA's 1982
pink-peppercorn import ban, Nyons as the first French olive appellation in 1994,
and Bella di Cerignola as the largest table olive in commerce. The génépi decree
is prefectoral, so Légifrance does not carry it — that one needs the Savoie
préfecture's own register.
